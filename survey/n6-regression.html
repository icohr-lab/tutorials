N6 · Tests and Regression

R for Survey Data, lesson 6 of 7 · about 45 minutes

In this lesson you will learn to:

  • Test whether two categorical variables are associated, accounting for the survey design
  • Fit a weighted logistic regression with svyglm()
  • Read odds ratios, before and after adjusting for other variables
  • See how ignoring the design can change a conclusion

Before this lesson: N5: Weighted Estimates. Work in n6.R in your starter project.

1. Setup

This lesson uses functions from the survey package directly, and broom (installed with the tidyverse) to tidy model results.

library(tidyverse)
library(srvyr)
library(survey)
library(broom)
analysis <- readRDS("data/nhis_analysis.rds")
nhis_design <- analysis %>%
  as_survey_design(ids = PPSU, strata = PSTRAT, weights = WTFA_A, nest = TRUE)

survivors <- nhis_design %>% filter(survivor == 1)

survivors is the design filtered to cancer survivors (the right way, from N5). Everything in this lesson uses it.

2. Testing an association: the design-based chi-square test

In N5 you saw that fair or poor health is much more common among survivors with lower incomes. Could a difference that large arise by chance in a sample this size? A chi-square test answers that for two categorical variables.

The ordinary chisq.test() assumes a simple random sample. svychisq() from the survey package uses the Rao-Scott correction, which accounts for weights, strata, and PSUs. It takes a formula naming the two variables, and the design.

svychisq(~ fair_poor + poverty, design = survivors)

    Pearson's X^2: Rao & Scott adjustment

data:  NextMethod()
F = 50.493, ndf = 2.9877, ddf = 1640.2560, p-value < 2.2e-16

How to read it:

  • F is the test statistic. Larger means stronger evidence of an association.
  • p-value: the probability of an association at least this strong if there really were none in the population. < 2.2e-16 is R’s way of saying “smaller than the smallest number it shows”, so report it as p < 0.001.

There is very strong evidence that self-rated health is associated with income among U.S. adult cancer survivors.

A large p-value does not prove that men and women have the same rate. It means this sample gives no evidence of a difference.

3. Why regression?

Income and education go together: people with more education tend to have higher incomes. So is the income gradient really about income, or partly about education? Regression estimates the association of each variable with the outcome while holding the others constant. This is called adjusting for them.

The outcome, fair_poor, is 0 or 1, so the model is logistic regression. Its results are odds ratios.

Odds and odds ratios

If 25% of a group report fair or poor health, their odds are 25 to 75, or 0.33. An odds ratio (OR) compares the odds in two groups:

  • OR = 1: same odds
  • OR = 2: twice the odds
  • OR = 0.5: half the odds

Every OR compares a group with a reference group. By default, R uses the first category of a factor. For poverty, that is “Below 100%”, which would give odds ratios below 1 for the other groups. Results are usually easier to read when the reference is the most advantaged group, so that every OR describes how much higher the odds are in a less advantaged group. fct_relevel() moves a category to the front.

survivors <- survivors %>%
  mutate(
    poverty = fct_relevel(poverty, "400% or more"),
    education = fct_relevel(education, "Bachelor or higher")
  )

4. A first model: income alone

svyglm() fits regression models on a survey design. It looks like glm(), with two differences: the data go in design =, and the family is quasibinomial(). (binomial() gives the same estimates but prints warnings about non-whole-number weights; quasibinomial() avoids them.)

model_crude <- svyglm(fair_poor ~ poverty, design = survivors, family = quasibinomial())

tidy(model_crude, exponentiate = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE) %>%
  select(term, estimate, conf.low, conf.high, p.value)
# A tibble: 4 × 5
  term              estimate conf.low conf.high   p.value
  <chr>                <dbl>    <dbl>     <dbl>     <dbl>
1 (Intercept)         0.2028   0.1695    0.2427 1.547e-54
2 povertyBelow 100%   6.698    4.555     9.849  1.347e-20
3 poverty100-199%     3.824    2.882     5.076  3.125e-19
4 poverty200-399%     1.865    1.433     2.427  4.206e- 6

tidy() from broom turns the model into a table. exponentiate = TRUE converts the model’s coefficients into odds ratios, and conf.int = TRUE adds 95% confidence intervals (conf.low, conf.high). select() keeps just the columns you need to read.

Read the estimate column. Compared with survivors whose family income is at least four times the poverty line, the odds of fair or poor health are about 1.9 times as high at 200-399%, 3.8 times as high at 100-199%, and 6.7 times as high below the poverty line. (Ignore the intercept row; it is the odds in the reference group, not a comparison.)

This is a crude (unadjusted) model: income alone.

5. Adjusting for other variables

Add education, age group, and sex to the formula with +.

NoteYour turn 2

Fit model_adjusted: fair_poor explained by poverty, education, age_group, and sex. Show its odds ratios with tidy(). How does the odds ratio for “Below 100%” change compared with the crude model?

Expected result: the odds ratio for income below the poverty line falls from 6.7 (95% CI 4.6 to 9.8) to 4.7 (3.1 to 7.2). It is smaller, but still large.

TipSolution 
model_adjusted <- svyglm(fair_poor ~ poverty + education + age_group + sex,
                         design = survivors, family = quasibinomial())

  <chr>                           <dbl>    <dbl>     <dbl>     <dbl>

2 povertyBelow 100%              4.717   3.098      7.181  1.461e-12

How to read the adjusted model:

  • Income: among survivors with the same education, age group, and sex, those below the poverty line have about 4.7 times the odds of fair or poor health of those at 400% or more. Part of the crude association was education; most of it remains.
  • Education: with income held constant, survivors with a high school education or less have about 2.5 times the odds of those with a bachelor’s degree. Income and education each matter.
  • Age group and sex: the confidence intervals include 1, so there is no clear evidence that either is associated with fair or poor health once income and education are accounted for.

The model used 2818 survivors: people with a missing value in any variable in the formula are left out automatically.

WarningOdds ratios are not risk ratios

An odds ratio of 4.7 does not mean “4.7 times as likely”. When an outcome is common (here more than one survivor in four), odds ratios are noticeably larger than ratios of percentages. In N5, the percentages were 57.6% and 16.9%, a ratio of about 3.4. Say “odds”, not “likelihood” or “risk”, when you report odds ratios.

6. Testing a whole variable

Poverty has three odds ratios. To ask one question, “is income associated with the outcome, after adjustment?”, test all three together with regTermTest():

regTermTest(model_adjusted, ~ poverty)
Wald test for poverty
 in svyglm(formula = fair_poor ~ poverty + education + age_group + 
    sex, design = survivors, family = quasibinomial())
F =  25.55712  on  3  and  541  df: p= 1.7848e-15

p < 0.001: strong evidence that income is associated with fair or poor health among survivors, independent of education, age, and sex.

7. What happens if you ignore the design?

Here is the same adjusted model fitted with ordinary glm(): no weights, no strata, no PSUs.

unweighted_model <- glm(
  fair_poor ~ poverty + education + age_group + sex,
  data = analysis %>%
    filter(survivor == 1) %>%
    mutate(
      poverty = fct_relevel(poverty, "400% or more"),
      education = fct_relevel(education, "Bachelor or higher")
    ),
  family = binomial()
)

tidy(unweighted_model, exponentiate = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE) %>%
  filter(term == "sexMale") %>%
  select(term, estimate, conf.low, conf.high, p.value)
# A tibble: 1 × 5
  term    estimate conf.low conf.high  p.value
  <chr>      <dbl>    <dbl>     <dbl>    <dbl>
1 sexMale    1.299    1.084     1.555 0.004510
NoteYour turn 3

Compare the sexMale row of unweighted_model with the same row of model_adjusted. Would the two models lead you to the same conclusion about sex?

Expected result: no. The unweighted model gives OR 1.30 with p = 0.005, which looks like clear evidence that men have higher odds. The design-based model gives OR 1.21 with p = 0.085 and a confidence interval that includes 1: no clear evidence.

TipSolution 
tidy(model_adjusted, exponentiate = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE) %>%
  filter(term == "sexMale") %>%
  select(term, estimate, conf.low, conf.high, p.value)
# A tibble: 1 × 5
  term    estimate conf.low conf.high p.value
  <chr>      <dbl>    <dbl>     <dbl>   <dbl>
1 sexMale    1.212   0.9740     1.508 0.08466

Ignoring the design changed both the estimate (the weights) and the uncertainty (the strata and PSUs), and together they turned “no clear evidence” into a “significant” finding. A paper built on the unweighted model would report a sex difference that the data do not support.

8. Reporting results

A typical results sentence:

Among U.S. adult cancer survivors, fair or poor self-rated health was strongly associated with family income. After adjustment for education, age, and sex, survivors with family income below the federal poverty line had 4.7 times the odds of fair or poor health of those with income at or above 400% of the poverty line (OR 4.72, 95% CI 3.10 to 7.18).

And in the methods: the survey-weighted logistic regression, the software (R with the survey and srvyr packages), and that variance was estimated using the NHIS strata and PSUs. Lesson N7 puts this together.

Recap

Code What it does
svychisq(~ a + b, design = d) Chi-square test with the Rao-Scott design correction
fct_relevel(x, "level") Make “level” the reference category
svyglm(y ~ x1 + x2, design = d, family = quasibinomial()) Survey-weighted logistic regression
tidy(model, exponentiate = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE) Odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals
regTermTest(model, ~ x) Test all categories of one variable together
