N4 · Why Weights Matter

R for Survey Data, lesson 4 of 7 · about 40 minutes

In this lesson you will learn to:

  • Explain what a survey weight is
  • Compare unweighted and weighted estimates
  • Set up the survey design with srvyr
  • Estimate a percentage with a 95% confidence interval that accounts for the design

Before this lesson: N3: Moving to RStudio. Work in n4.R in your starter project.

1. Setup

Start n4.R with these lines. Every lesson script from now on starts the same way.

library(tidyverse)
library(srvyr)

analysis <- readRDS("data/nhis_analysis.rds")

If readRDS() says the file does not exist, run prepare.R first (N3, section 6).

2. What a weight is

Each adult in the file has a weight, WTFA_A: the number of U.S. adults that person stands for.

summary(analysis$WTFA_A)
   Min. 1st Qu.  Median    Mean 3rd Qu.    Max. 
   2358    5835    9020   10936   13371   45481

The median person stands for about 9,000 adults, but weights range from about 2,400 to 45,000. Someone with a weight of 45,000 belongs to a group that was hard to reach or less likely to respond, so each person interviewed from that group has to stand for many more people. Someone with a small weight belongs to a group that was easy to reach.

Add up all the weights with sum(). What does the total represent?

Expected result: 264,821,349, about 265 million: the number of U.S. adults the survey represents (adults living in households, not in institutions such as nursing homes or prisons).

TipSolution

[1] 264821349

3. Unweighted versus weighted

How many U.S. adults are 65 or older? Here is the share of the sample, and the share once each person counts as many people as their weight says. weighted.mean() does that.

analysis %>%
  filter(!is.na(age_group)) %>%
  summarize(
    unweighted = 100 * mean(age_group == "65+"),
    weighted   = 100 * weighted.mean(age_group == "65+", WTFA_A)
  )
# A tibble: 1 × 2
  unweighted weighted
       <dbl>    <dbl>
1      35.06    23.26

More than a third of the people interviewed are 65 or older, but they make up less than a quarter of U.S. adults. Older adults are more likely to live alone or with just one other adult (and only one adult per household is interviewed), and they are more likely to take part, so the sample has too many of them. Each of them gets a smaller weight to make up for it.

This matters for the project because cancer is much more common at older ages. Any unweighted estimate about survivors leans toward older people.

NoteYour turn 2

What percent of U.S. adults are cancer survivors? Calculate it unweighted and weighted, leaving out people with missing survivor.

Expected result: 11.7% unweighted, 8.7% weighted.

TipSolution 
analysis %>%
  filter(!is.na(survivor)) %>%
  summarize(
    unweighted = 100 * mean(survivor),
    weighted   = 100 * weighted.mean(survivor, WTFA_A)
  )
# A tibble: 1 × 2
  unweighted weighted
       <dbl>    <dbl>
1      11.72    8.703

4. Why weights are not enough

weighted.mean() gives the right estimate. It cannot tell you how certain that estimate is, and every estimate from a sample needs a measure of uncertainty, usually a 95% confidence interval.

Uncertainty depends on how the sample was drawn, and the NHIS was not drawn one person at a time:

  • Clusters (sampling units). The NHIS first picks geographic areas, then households within them. People in the same area tend to be alike, so 30 people from one area tell you less than 30 people from 30 different areas. The column PPSU records each person’s primary sampling unit (PSU).
  • Strata. The country is first divided into groups of areas called strata, and areas are picked within each one. This makes estimates more precise. The column PSTRAT records the stratum.

Software that ignores clusters treats everyone as independent, and its confidence intervals come out too narrow: they claim more certainty than the data support.

5. The survey design object

The srvyr package stores the data together with the design in one object. Everything after this works on that object instead of on analysis.

nhis_design <- analysis %>%
  as_survey_design(
    ids = PPSU,          # primary sampling units (clusters)
    strata = PSTRAT,     # strata
    weights = WTFA_A,    # weights
    nest = TRUE          # PSU numbers restart within each stratum
  )

nhis_design
Stratified 1 - level Cluster Sampling design (with replacement)
With (608) clusters.
Called via srvyr
Sampling variables:
  - ids: PPSU 
  - strata: PSTRAT 
  - weights: WTFA_A 
Data variables: 
  - HHX (chr), WTFA_A (dbl), PSTRAT (dbl), PPSU (dbl), fair_poor (dbl),
    survivor (dbl), poverty (fct), education (fct), age (dbl), age_group (chr),
    sex (chr), insured (chr)

Each argument names a column from the file. These four lines come straight from NCHS’s own instructions for analyzing the NHIS in R, in the 2025 survey description.

NoteWhy nest = TRUE?

PSU numbers are only unique within a stratum: two different areas in two different strata can share a number.

n_distinct(analysis$PPSU)                     # PSU numbers
[1] 606
nrow(distinct(analysis, PSTRAT, PPSU))        # actual PSUs
[1] 608

nest = TRUE tells R to identify a PSU by its stratum and its number. Without it, R would merge areas that are really separate.

6. Estimates with confidence intervals

Use summarize() on the design object, with survey_mean() in place of mean(). vartype = "ci" asks for a 95% confidence interval, and na.rm = TRUE leaves out missing values.

nhis_design %>%
  summarize(fair_poor = survey_mean(fair_poor, na.rm = TRUE, vartype = "ci"))
# A tibble: 1 × 3
  fair_poor fair_poor_low fair_poor_upp
      <dbl>         <dbl>         <dbl>
1    0.1472        0.1409        0.1534

survey_mean() gives a proportion. About 14.7% of U.S. adults report fair or poor health, and the 95% confidence interval runs from 14.1% to 15.3%. (The unweighted figure would have been 15.8%.)

group_by() works on the design just as it does on a dataframe.

NoteYour turn 3

Estimate the percent in fair or poor health for survivors and non-survivors, with 95% confidence intervals. Start from nhis_design, remove missing survivor with filter(), then use group_by() and survey_mean(). Multiply by 100 with mutate() if you like.

Expected result: survivors 27.4% (95% CI 25.3% to 29.5%); non-survivors 13.5% (12.9% to 14.1%).

TipSolution 
nhis_design %>%
  filter(!is.na(survivor)) %>%
  group_by(survivor) %>%
  summarize(fair_poor = survey_mean(fair_poor, na.rm = TRUE, vartype = "ci")) %>%
  mutate(across(starts_with("fair_poor"), ~ 100 * .x))
# A tibble: 2 × 4
  survivor fair_poor fair_poor_low fair_poor_upp
     <dbl>     <dbl>         <dbl>         <dbl>
1        0     13.50         12.87         14.14
2        1     27.42         25.34         29.51

across(starts_with("fair_poor"), ~ 100 * .x) multiplies every column whose name starts with fair_poor (the estimate and both limits) by 100.

Among survivors, the weighted estimate (27.4%) is close to the unweighted one from N2 (26.9%). Weighting does not always change an estimate much, as here, but sometimes it does, as with age. You only know by doing it.

7. Seeing what the design does

Here is the same estimate, fair or poor health among survivors, calculated three ways.

# 1. Ignoring the design: a simple confidence interval for a proportion
surv <- analysis %>% filter(survivor == 1, !is.na(fair_poor))
p <- mean(surv$fair_poor)
se <- sqrt(p * (1 - p) / nrow(surv))
c(estimate = p, low = p - 1.96 * se, high = p + 1.96 * se) * 100
estimate      low     high 
26.88096 25.24782 28.51410 
# 2. Weights only
analysis %>%
  as_survey_design(weights = WTFA_A) %>%
  filter(survivor == 1) %>%
  summarize(fair_poor = survey_mean(fair_poor, na.rm = TRUE, vartype = "ci") * 100)
# A tibble: 1 × 3
  fair_poor fair_poor_low fair_poor_upp
      <dbl>         <dbl>         <dbl>
1     27.42         25.45         29.39
# 3. The full design
nhis_design %>%
  filter(survivor == 1) %>%
  summarize(fair_poor = survey_mean(fair_poor, na.rm = TRUE, vartype = "ci") * 100)
# A tibble: 1 × 3
  fair_poor fair_poor_low fair_poor_upp
      <dbl>         <dbl>         <dbl>
1     27.42         25.34         29.51
Method Estimate 95% CI Width
Ignoring the design 26.9% 25.2 to 28.5 3.3
Weights only 27.4% 25.5 to 29.4 3.9
Full design 27.4% 25.3 to 29.5 4.2
  • The weights fix the estimate (26.9% becomes 27.4%).
  • The strata and PSUs fix the uncertainty: the honest interval is about a quarter wider than the naive one.

The difference here is modest. For smaller groups and for variables that cluster strongly by place, such as income, it can be much larger. Since you cannot know in advance, always use the full design.

8. From percentages to people

Weights also turn counts of people in the sample into counts of people in the country. survey_total() adds up the weights.

NoteYour turn 4

How many U.S. adults are cancer survivors? Use group_by(survivor) and survey_total(vartype = "ci") on nhis_design. Dividing by 1e6 (one million) makes the numbers easier to read.

Expected result: about 23.0 million survivors (95% CI 21.8 to 24.2 million). The other rows are non-survivors and people with missing survivor.

TipSolution 
nhis_design %>%
  group_by(survivor) %>%
  summarize(adults = survey_total(vartype = "ci")) %>%
  mutate(across(starts_with("adults"), ~ .x / 1e6))   # in millions
# A tibble: 3 × 4
  survivor   adults adults_low adults_upp
     <dbl>    <dbl>      <dbl>      <dbl>
1        0 241.5      233.8      249.2   
2        1  23.02      21.83      24.22  
3       NA   0.2731     0.1688     0.3774

2,833 people in the sample stand for about 23 million U.S. adults. (Remember the definition from N2: non-melanoma skin cancer alone does not count.)

Recap

Code What it does
weighted.mean(x, w) Weighted mean or proportion, without uncertainty
as_survey_design(ids = PPSU, strata = PSTRAT, weights = WTFA_A, nest = TRUE) The NHIS design object
survey_mean(x, na.rm = TRUE, vartype = "ci") Weighted mean or proportion with a 95% CI
survey_total(vartype = "ci") Estimated number of people, with a 95% CI
group_by() on the design Estimates for each group

The rule: weights for the estimate; strata and PSUs for the uncertainty; always all three.
